How to Budget on a Low Income in the UK

When money is tight, a budget needs to be realistic, flexible and focused on what matters most. This guide explains how to map your income, prioritise essential costs, plan food and travel spending, and find help if the numbers still don't add up.

Budgeting on a low income is not about finding endless little luxuries to cut or blaming yourself when prices rise. Sometimes the essentials simply take up most of what comes in. A clear plan can still help you understand what is due, spot a shortfall earlier and make informed choices about the money available.

Start here: a five-step checklist
  1. Write down the income you can reliably expect and the dates it arrives.
  2. List essential bills and priority payments with their due dates.
  3. Plan food, travel, childcare and other everyday essentials.
  4. Check for irregular costs and any likely shortfall before payday.
  5. If you cannot cover essentials or priority bills, contact the relevant provider and seek free, impartial advice early.

1. Build your budget around money you can rely on

Use take-home pay after tax and deductions, plus benefits or other income you are confident you will receive. If your hours or earnings vary, avoid building the plan around your best month. Use a cautious estimate based on recent income, then make a separate plan for any extra money when it arrives.

Write down payment dates as well as amounts. A monthly total can look manageable while the timing is difficult, especially if rent, energy or travel costs are due before the next payday. If you are paid weekly or every four weeks, map the actual dates rather than assuming each calendar month is identical.

2. Prioritise essential bills and payments

List the costs that protect your home, basic services, ability to work and family wellbeing. These commonly include housing, council tax, energy, water, food, essential travel, childcare and relevant insurance. Which payments are most urgent depends on your circumstances and the consequences of missing them.

Not all debts have the same consequences. If you cannot pay everything, do not simply pay the creditor who contacts you most often. Get advice on which payments are priority debts and what options may be available. Do not ignore letters, court papers or notices about possible loss of housing or essential services.

Important: If you are at risk of missing rent, mortgage, council tax, energy or other essential payments, contact the provider or a free debt-advice service promptly. Explain what you can realistically afford; do not promise payments that leave you without essentials.

3. Work out a realistic food and household budget

Food is essential, but the right amount depends on household size, ages, dietary needs, location and what you include in the shop. Check recent receipts and bank transactions before choosing a weekly target. Include packed lunches, drinks, toiletries and cleaning products if they come from the same pot, or track those categories separately so you can see where the money goes.

Planning a few meals around food already in the cupboard or freezer, writing a shopping list and checking unit prices can help. Frozen and tinned ingredients may be useful alternatives to fresh food, and batch cooking can make ingredients go further if you have the time, storage and facilities. Avoid buying larger packs just because the unit price is lower if some will be wasted.

Our guide to setting a realistic weekly food budget has more practical planning ideas. The free Family Money Planner food budget tool can help you keep track of a weekly allowance.

4. Plan travel, work and childcare costs

Include the costs that make it possible to earn or receive income: travel to work, school transport where needed, childcare, work clothing, phone access and other necessary expenses. If a cost varies, use a realistic average and keep an eye on weeks when it is higher. Check whether an employer, local council or benefit scheme offers support that may apply to your situation.

5. Prepare for irregular expenses, even in small amounts

Car repairs, school uniforms, birthdays, seasonal heating costs and annual bills can knock a tight budget off course. If you can, estimate the annual total and divide it into a monthly amount. If there is no room to save right now, keep a list of upcoming costs and their dates so they are not a complete surprise.

Do not put money into savings at the expense of essential bills or priority payments. A small buffer can be helpful when it is affordable, but getting the essentials covered comes first.

6. Check whether you are receiving the support you are entitled to

If your income is low, it may be worth checking whether you qualify for benefits, help with housing costs, Council Tax Reduction, free school meals, childcare support or other local assistance. Eligibility rules depend on your circumstances and can change, so use an official or reputable benefits calculator rather than relying on a general article.

  • GOV.UK benefits calculators – independent calculators to check what support you may be entitled to.
  • MoneyHelper – free, impartial guidance on benefits, budgeting, bills and debt.
  • Citizens Advice – information and advice on benefits, housing, bills and debt.

For help with energy costs, check your supplier's support options and the latest official guidance. Be wary of anyone who asks for an upfront fee to access benefits or grants on your behalf.

7. If your budget does not balance, take action early

If essential spending is higher than reliable income, a calculator cannot solve the shortfall by itself. Check the figures, make sure you have included all income and bills, and identify what is due first. Contact providers before missing payments where possible and ask what support or affordable arrangements they can offer.

Free debt advice organisations can help you understand options and make a plan. In England and Wales, you can contact StepChange or National Debtline. If you are in Scotland or Northern Ireland, check services appropriate to where you live. You do not need to wait until a situation becomes urgent to ask for help.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best way to budget on a low income?

Start with reliable take-home income and payment dates. Prioritise essential costs, list other commitments, plan everyday spending and identify any shortfall early. Make the plan reflect your actual circumstances rather than forcing it into a generic percentage rule.

Should I save money if I have debt?

It depends on the type of debt, interest, arrears and your essential needs. Do not build savings by missing priority payments. A free debt adviser can help you decide what to prioritise and whether a small emergency buffer is realistic.

What should I do if I cannot afford my bills?

Contact the relevant providers as early as possible, explain what you can afford and seek free, impartial debt advice. If you are at risk of losing your home or essential services, make that clear when asking for help.

Can I get help with food or household costs?

Depending on where you live and your circumstances, support may be available through your local council, schools, charities or community organisations. Citizens Advice and your local council can help you find current options. Check eligibility and avoid services that demand suspicious upfront fees.
