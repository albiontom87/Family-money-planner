How to Set a Realistic Weekly Food Budget for Your Family

A food budget should help you feel in control, not leave you constantly counting pennies. Start with an amount your household can realistically stick to, a simple meal plan and a little room for everyday surprises.

This guide is general budgeting information. Supermarket prices and household needs vary, so use the examples as a starting point rather than a fixed rule.

1. Decide what your food budget covers

Before choosing a weekly limit, decide what counts as “food shopping” in your household. You might include breakfasts, lunches, dinners, snacks, packed lunches, drinks and top-up shops. If toiletries and cleaning products come from the same supermarket shop, include them in your overall plan but track them separately where possible. A big bottle of detergent should not make it look as though you have overspent on food.

2. Start with your actual spending

Look at the last three or four weeks of supermarket receipts or bank transactions. Estimate how much went on food, household essentials, takeaways and top-up shops. Use an average as your starting point rather than picking an ambitious number at random.

Example: If your household currently spends around £150 a week, dropping straight to £80 may be difficult. A first target of £135–£140 could be more sustainable, then review it after a few weeks.

3. Make a simple weekly meal plan

  1. Check cupboards, fridge and freezer before shopping.
  2. Choose meals that use ingredients you already have.
  3. Plan a couple of flexible meals for leftovers or freezer food.
  4. Write a shopping list and group items by supermarket section.
  5. Check the list before checkout and remove items you do not need this week.

You do not need seven completely different dinners. Repeating ingredients in different meals can reduce waste and make shopping easier.

4. Example weekly budget for a family of four

There is no single correct amount for every family. Age, appetite, dietary needs, location and what you already have at home all affect the total. This hypothetical £120 plan shows one way to organise a budget; it is not a claim about current average supermarket prices.

CategoryExample allocation
Breakfasts and lunch items£28
Dinners and ingredients£48
Fruit, vegetables and snacks£20
Milk, bread and top-up essentials£12
Buffer for price changes£12
Total£120

If cleaning products and toiletries also come from this pot, add a separate allowance or increase the total accordingly. Do not force the food allowance to cover more than your budget can realistically manage.

5. Turn the weekly limit into a daily guide

Dividing your weekly amount by seven gives a simple daily equivalent. For a £120 budget, that is about £17.14 per day for the household. This is a planning guide, not a rule that you must spend the same amount every day.

You might spend £85 on the main shop and use the remaining £35 for fresh items and planned top-ups. The key is to track the total for the week, rather than judge each day in isolation.

6. Five ways to make the budget go further

  • Use what you already own. Check the freezer and cupboard before planning meals.
  • Keep a few low-effort meals. A reliable store-cupboard dinner can prevent an expensive takeaway.
  • Compare unit prices. The largest pack is not always best value if some will go to waste.
  • Freeze food you will not use in time. Label leftovers and include them in a future meal plan.
  • Review top-up trips. Small unplanned purchases can add up, so note them for a couple of weeks.

7. Review the budget after a few weeks

At the end of each week, compare your planned amount with what you actually spent. If you consistently overspend, work out why before cutting the budget again. The target may be unrealistic, or your plan may not account for packed lunches, household products or extra guests. Make one change at a time, then see whether it helps.

Frequently asked questions

What is a reasonable weekly food budget for a family of four in the UK?

It depends on your household's needs, where you shop, dietary requirements and whether toiletries or cleaning products are included. Track your current spending first, then set a realistic target for your circumstances.

Should toiletries and cleaning products be included?

You can include them in one supermarket budget, but track them separately if possible. This makes it easier to understand your true food costs and adjust each part.

Is a daily food budget useful if I shop weekly?

Yes, as a rough guide. It helps you understand the weekly amount, but you do not need to spend evenly each day.

What if I keep going over budget?

Review a few weeks of spending and identify the main cause. Adjust the target to a realistic level, plan meals around what you have and reduce avoidable waste before making another cut.
