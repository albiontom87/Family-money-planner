UK household budgeting

How to Create a Family Budget in the UK

A practical, no-fuss way to plan household income, bills, food shopping and the money you have left until payday. You don't need a complicated spreadsheet or to get every number perfect on day one.

A family budget is simply a plan for where your household money needs to go. It can help you spot upcoming shortfalls, decide what is affordable and make room for savings where possible. The aim isn't to judge every purchase; it's to make the month more predictable.

Quick summary
  • Start with take-home income, not gross salary.
  • List regular bills and essential spending first.
  • Allow for annual and irregular costs by setting aside a monthly amount.
  • Set a realistic weekly food and everyday-spending allowance.
  • Review the plan after a month and adjust it to real life.

1. Work out the money coming in

Use the amount that actually reaches your bank account each month. Include reliable household income that you want to budget for, such as a partner's take-home pay or regular benefits. If income changes from month to month, build the plan around a cautious, dependable figure rather than assuming every month will be a good one.

For weekly or four-weekly pay, convert it carefully to a monthly average. For example, weekly income multiplied by 52 and divided by 12 gives a monthly average. Keep a note of the pay dates too, because the timing of bills and wages matters as much as the monthly total.

2. List your regular bills and essentials

Check recent bank statements and bills so you don't rely on memory. Common categories include:

  • Mortgage or rent and council tax
  • Gas, electricity, water, broadband and mobile phones
  • Food, toiletries and household cleaning products
  • Travel, fuel, parking and public transport
  • Childcare, school costs and other family essentials
  • Minimum debt repayments and other contractual payments

Use actual amounts where you can. If a bill changes during the year, estimate a sensible monthly average and review it when you have a new statement.

3. Make room for irregular expenses

One reason budgets go off track is that some costs don't arrive every month. Examples include car servicing, birthdays, school uniforms, Christmas, annual insurance, home repairs and family days out.

Add up what you expect to spend over a year and divide by 12. If you expect to spend £600 on car maintenance and annual motoring costs, for example, setting aside £50 a month gives that cost a place in the plan. The estimate won't be perfect, but it is usually better than treating these bills as surprises.

Helpful rule: if a cost is predictable but not monthly, it still belongs in your budget. Give it a monthly allowance.

4. Set a realistic food budget

Choose a weekly amount that fits your household and include everything paid from that pot. For some families that means groceries and packed lunches; for others it also includes toiletries, cleaning products and pet food. Decide what is included so you're comparing like with like.

Look at what you've actually spent over the last few weeks before choosing a target. If the target is too ambitious, lower it gradually rather than planning a figure that is impossible to maintain. A simple meal plan, checking cupboards before shopping and using a list can help reduce waste.

5. Decide what to do with the money left

Subtract planned costs from household take-home income. If the result is positive, check whether you have allowed for irregular bills before treating it as spare money. You might use some for a buffer, savings, extra debt repayments or family spending, depending on your circumstances.

If the result is negative, your plan currently spends more than comes in. Start by checking that the figures are accurate, then separate essential costs from things you can change. Avoid assuming that you can cut every flexible expense to zero. If you are struggling to keep up with priority bills or debts, consider getting free, impartial debt advice from a reputable UK service such as MoneyHelper or StepChange.

6. Make a plan for payday

Write down when income arrives and when the major bills leave your account. If possible, set aside money for bills and planned costs soon after payday. Then decide what is available for food and everyday spending until the next payday.

A monthly budget can look healthy on paper but still feel tight if bills leave the account before wages arrive. A simple calendar of paydays and bill dates can help you see these timing gaps early.

7. Review the budget after one month

A budget is a working plan, not a test you pass or fail. At the end of the month, compare your plan with what happened. Which costs were higher than expected? Did you forget an annual expense? Was the food allowance realistic? Adjust the figures and try again.

Keep the process manageable. A short weekly check and a more thorough monthly review are often more useful than trying to track every penny perfectly every day.

Frequently asked questions

What is the easiest way to start a family budget?

Start with take-home income, the bills you must pay, essential spending and any predictable irregular costs. Then calculate what's left and make a realistic plan for it. You can improve the detail over time.

How much should a family spend on food each week?

There isn't one suitable amount for every family. Household size, dietary needs, location and whether toiletries or cleaning products are included all affect the total. Start with your actual recent spending and adjust gradually.

What if my expenses are higher than my income?

First check that the figures and timing are correct. Identify which costs are essential and whether any payments can be reviewed. If you're missing priority payments or struggling with debt, seek free, impartial support rather than relying on a calculator alone.

Do I need a budgeting app?

No. Paper, a spreadsheet or a simple calculator can be enough. Choose a method you can maintain and understand, and be careful about where you store personal financial information.
