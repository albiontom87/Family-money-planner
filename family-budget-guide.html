UK household budgeting

How to Create a Family Budget in the UK

Want to know how to budget your family income, cover the bills and make your money last until payday? This practical guide takes you through a simple household budget, one step at a time. You don't need a complicated spreadsheet or perfect figures to get started.

A family budget is a plan for the money coming into your household and the costs that need to go out. It can help you see what is affordable, prepare for bills that don't arrive every month and decide what to do with any money left over. The aim isn't to judge every purchase; it's to make the month more predictable.

Quick family budget checklist
  • Work out your household's monthly take-home income.
  • List housing, bills, food, travel and other essential costs.
  • Include debt repayments and regular commitments.
  • Set aside money each month for annual and unexpected costs.
  • Decide what is available for savings and flexible spending.
  • Review your plan after a month and adjust it to real life.

1. Work out your monthly household income

Start with the money your household actually receives, rather than gross salaries before tax. Include reliable income you intend to use in the budget, such as both adults' take-home pay and regular benefits. If your income changes, use a cautious figure you can depend on instead of budgeting around an unusually good month.

If you are paid weekly, multiply your usual take-home pay by 52 and divide by 12 to estimate a monthly average. If you are paid every four weeks, multiply by 13 and divide by 12. Remember that four-weekly pay dates move through the calendar, so a monthly average is useful for planning but doesn't replace checking when money will actually arrive.

Keep irregular overtime, commission or bonuses separate until you know they are available. You can then decide whether to use extra income for a buffer, savings or a particular goal.

2. List your household bills and essential spending

Check bank statements, bills and renewal notices so you don't rely on memory. A household budget planner might use categories such as:

  • Home: mortgage or rent, council tax and buildings or contents insurance.
  • Utilities and communication: gas, electricity, water, broadband and mobile phones.
  • Food and household essentials: groceries, packed lunches, toiletries and cleaning products.
  • Travel: fuel, public transport, parking, vehicle tax and insurance.
  • Family: childcare, school costs, clothing, activities and subscriptions.
  • Borrowing: minimum credit card, loan and other debt repayments.

Use actual amounts where possible. For bills that change during the year, estimate a sensible monthly average and update it when you receive a new statement. Make sure you don't count the same cost in two categories.

3. Include annual and irregular family expenses

One reason a budget can look fine on paper but still run short is that some costs don't arrive every month. Examples include car servicing, birthdays, school uniforms, Christmas, annual insurance, home repairs and family days out.

Estimate what each of these costs over a year and divide by 12. For example, if you expect to spend £600 across the year on car maintenance, setting aside £50 a month gives that expense a place in your budget. The estimate won't be perfect, but it is usually better than treating predictable costs as surprises.

Helpful rule: if a cost is predictable but not monthly, it still belongs in your household budget. Give it a monthly allowance, even if you keep the money in a separate savings pot.

4. Set a realistic weekly food budget

Choose a weekly amount that fits your household and be clear about what it covers. Some families include groceries and packed lunches; others also pay for toiletries, cleaning products and pet food from the same pot. Decide what is included so you can compare your spending fairly from week to week.

Look at what you've actually spent over the last few weeks before choosing a target. If the amount feels too high, reduce it gradually rather than setting a figure that is impossible to maintain. Planning a few meals, checking cupboards before shopping and using a list can help reduce waste.

For more detailed ideas, read our guide to setting a realistic weekly food budget for your family. You can also use the food budget calculator on the free Family Money Planner homepage to divide a weekly allowance across the days ahead.

5. Calculate what is left after your planned spending

Add up your planned monthly costs and subtract them from your household take-home income. If the result is positive, check that you have included annual bills and irregular costs before treating it as spare money. You might use some for an emergency buffer, savings, extra debt repayments or family spending, depending on your circumstances.

If the result is negative, your current plan spends more than comes in. First check that the figures are accurate and that you haven't counted anything twice. Then separate essential costs from things you may be able to change. Avoid assuming that every flexible expense can simply be cut to zero. If you are struggling to keep up with priority bills or debts, consider free, impartial support from a reputable UK service such as MoneyHelper or StepChange.

6. Make a plan for payday

Write down when income arrives and when the major bills leave your account. If possible, set aside money for bills and planned costs soon after payday. Then decide what is available for food and everyday spending until the next payday.

A monthly budget can look healthy on paper but still feel tight if bills leave the account before wages arrive. A simple calendar of paydays and bill dates can help you spot timing gaps early. If you are paid weekly or four-weekly, plan around the actual dates rather than assuming every month will look the same.

7. Review your family budget every month

A budget is a working plan, not a test you pass or fail. At the end of the month, compare your plan with what happened. Which costs were higher than expected? Did you forget an annual expense? Was the food allowance realistic? Adjust the figures and try again.

Keep the process manageable. A short weekly check and a more thorough monthly review are often more useful than trying to track every penny perfectly every day. If a budget category is regularly too low, change the plan to reflect real spending rather than repeatedly setting an unrealistic target.

Example monthly family budget

This example shows how a household with £3,200 monthly take-home income might organise its spending. It is illustrative only, not a recommendation or a guide to what every family should spend. Your costs will depend on housing, location, household size, travel and personal circumstances.

Frequently asked questions about family budgeting

What is the easiest way to start a family budget?

Start with monthly take-home income, the bills you must pay, essential spending and predictable irregular costs. Then calculate what's left and make a realistic plan for it. You can improve the detail over time.

How do you budget family income when you are paid weekly?

For a monthly average, multiply weekly take-home income by 52 and divide by 12. Then use your actual payday dates to plan when bills can be paid and how much is available between paydays.

What should be included in a household budget?

Include housing, council tax, utilities, food, transport, childcare, debt repayments, subscriptions, family costs and money set aside for annual or irregular expenses. Include savings or an emergency buffer if your circumstances allow.

How much should a family spend on food each week?

There isn't one suitable amount for every family. Household size, dietary needs, location and whether toiletries or cleaning products are included all affect the total. Start with your actual recent spending and adjust gradually.

What if my expenses are higher than my income?

First check that the figures and timing are correct. Identify which costs are essential and whether any payments can be reviewed. If you're missing priority payments or struggling with debt, seek free, impartial support rather than relying on a calculator alone.

Do I need a budgeting app or spreadsheet?

No. Paper, a spreadsheet or a simple calculator can be enough. Choose a method you can maintain and understand, and be careful about where you store personal financial information.
