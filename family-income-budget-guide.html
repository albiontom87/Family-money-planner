Family budgeting guide

How to Budget Your Family Income in the UK

A family budget should make everyday money decisions easier, not leave you feeling guilty every time something costs more than expected. The aim is to give your household income a clear job: cover essentials, plan for family life and make progress towards the things that matter.

This guide walks through a straightforward monthly method for families in the UK, whether your household has one income, two incomes, benefits, overtime or a mixture.

Start with your take-home income. Use the amount that actually reaches your bank account after tax, National Insurance, pension contributions and other deductions. This gives you a realistic figure to plan around.

1. Work out the income your family can rely on

List each regular source of household income and the date it normally arrives. This might include wages, self-employment income, Child Benefit, Universal Credit or other support. Keep irregular overtime, commission and bonuses separate from guaranteed income until they arrive.

If income changes from month to month, build your essential budget around a cautious figure rather than your best month. When extra money comes in, decide in advance how much will go towards overdue costs, savings, debt or family treats.

2. Put essential bills and commitments first

Write down rent or mortgage payments, Council Tax, energy, water, broadband, phone, insurance, travel to work, childcare and minimum debt repayments. Include the due date and whether each cost is fixed or likely to change.

Prioritise payments that protect your home, essential services and ability to work. If you are struggling to pay a bill, contact the provider or a free debt-advice organisation early rather than ignoring letters.

3. Give food and everyday spending a realistic allowance

Set a weekly food and household-shopping amount that reflects the people in your home, packed lunches, toiletries and cleaning products. Multiply the weekly amount by 52 and divide by 12 if you want a monthly equivalent; simply multiplying by four will understate the annual cost.

Allow separate amounts for fuel or public transport, school costs, children’s activities, prescriptions where relevant, clothing and small day-to-day purchases. A budget only works if it reflects real life.

4. Plan for costs that do not happen every month

Birthdays, Christmas, school uniforms, car maintenance, MOTs, insurance renewals, holidays and home repairs can derail a budget when they arrive unexpectedly. They are irregular, but they are not surprises.

Estimate the annual cost of each item and divide by 12. Put that amount aside each month in a separate savings pot if you can. Start with the expenses most likely to cause stress if they land all at once.

5. Make a simple monthly family budget

Here is an illustrative example for a household with £3,200 monthly take-home income. It is not a recommended split: housing, childcare, transport and local costs vary widely.

The buffer is there for small changes and forgotten costs. If your budget has no room at all, do not assume you have failed: use the figures to identify which costs are fixed, which can be changed and where you may need support.

6. Match the budget to your payday dates

A monthly budget can look balanced on paper and still leave you short if several direct debits leave before payday. List the dates income arrives and bills are collected. If a bill date causes a regular squeeze, ask the provider whether it can be moved.

For weekly or four-weekly pay, plan around the actual payment calendar. Some months will contain an extra weekly payday; decide how to use it before it arrives instead of treating it as money with no purpose.

7. Review it briefly every week

Spend ten minutes checking what has gone out, what is still due and how much remains for food and flexible spending. At the end of the month, adjust allowances that were unrealistic. A budget is a working plan, not a test you pass or fail.

What if your family income does not cover everything?

Focus first on immediate essentials and priority commitments. Contact creditors and providers if you cannot pay, and seek free, independent help. In the UK, you can use MoneyHelper, Citizens Advice, StepChange or National Debtline. If your circumstances have changed, check whether you may be entitled to support using a reputable benefits calculator such as GOV.UK's benefits calculators.

Frequently asked questions

What percentage of income should a family spend on bills?

There is no single percentage that works for every family. Housing, childcare, energy and travel costs differ. Start with your actual essential costs, then see what remains for food, irregular expenses, savings and flexible spending.

Should I budget monthly or weekly?

Use a monthly plan for income and bills, then break flexible categories such as food and spending into weekly limits. This makes it easier to manage the gap between paydays.

How much should a family save each month?

Save an amount that is sustainable after essential costs. Even a small regular amount can help build a buffer, but if you are behind on priority bills or struggling with expensive debt, getting free advice may be the better first step.

What is the easiest way to start?

Write down take-home income, list essential bills and due dates, then estimate food and transport. Use the remaining amount to plan irregular costs and flexible spending. Review the plan after your first month.
